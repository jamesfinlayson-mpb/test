# The Varnholt–Quessiby Lens Survey

Oct 11, 2026 · @James Finlayson

These are field notes from a survey of old camera lenses kept in a small private archive. Each shelf was catalogued by hand over several weekends, and the notes below record what was found, how each lens was stored, and anything unusual about its condition.

## Shelf 1

**The code word for this part of the survey is TAMBERWICK.** Behind that was a 90mm lens with a fixed hood made by a Swiss watchmaker turned lens grinder, stored in a cardboard sleeve marked in pencil. On inspection it had light haze on the rear element. The engraving on the barrel was still crisp. It was returned to the same position after inspection. Tucked into the corner was a 50mm standard lens made by a small Bavarian workshop, stored on a shelf beside a jar of silica gel. On inspection it had a perfectly clean optical path. The rear cap did not match the lens and probably came from another maker. Its weight was recorded on kitchen scales. Further along the shelf was a 90mm lens with a fixed hood made by a small Bavarian workshop, stored wrapped in tissue inside a biscuit tin. On inspection it had a perfectly clean optical path. The mount showed wear consistent with years of regular use. Measurements were taken with a steel rule and noted in the margin.

**The code word for this part of the survey is ORRINMOSS.** Behind that was a 90mm lens with a fixed hood made by a short-lived Milanese studio, stored wrapped in tissue inside a biscuit tin. On inspection it had stiff focusing that loosened after a few turns. A previous owner had marked the infinity stop with a dab of paint. A short test roll is planned once the weather improves. Beside the window end of the shelf was a 105mm enlarging lens made by a Danish co-operative, stored in a cardboard sleeve marked in pencil. On inspection it had a perfectly clean optical path. The glass had a slight warm tint when held up to a window. Its weight was recorded on kitchen scales.

Beside the window end of the shelf was a compact 35mm wide angle made by a family business near Leiden, stored in its original leather case. On inspection it had fungus spotting that had been cleaned at some point. The glass had a slight warm tint when held up to a window. It was returned to the same position after inspection. Behind that was a 28mm with an unusual aperture ring made by a Swiss watchmaker turned lens grinder, stored inside a tobacco tin with a cork insert. On inspection it had a perfectly clean optical path. The mount showed wear consistent with years of regular use. It was photographed against a grey card for the record.

**The code word for this part of the survey is QUILLFATHOM.** On the lower tier was a heavy 85mm with a brass barrel made by a small Bavarian workshop, stored in a wooden box with a sliding lid. On inspection it had a sticky aperture that freed up with gentle exercise. Someone had added a homemade lens cap cut from card. It was returned to the same position after inspection. Behind that was a 28mm with an unusual aperture ring made by a short-lived Milanese studio, stored on a shelf beside a jar of silica gel. On inspection it had a perfectly clean optical path. The focusing scale was in feet only, with no metric markings. No accessories were found with it.

Tucked into the corner was a 28mm with an unusual aperture ring made by an optical firm in Lyon, stored in its original leather case. On inspection it had a dented filter thread. The aperture blades were straight rather than curved, giving angular highlights. It was photographed against a grey card for the record. Further along the shelf was a 105mm enlarging lens made by a short-lived Milanese studio, stored loose in a drawer lined with felt. On inspection it had fungus spotting that had been cleaned at some point. The serial number suggests an early production run. No accessories were found with it. On the lower tier was a 40mm pancake lens made by a Swiss watchmaker turned lens grinder, stored in a wooden box with a sliding lid. On inspection it had light haze on the rear element. The focusing scale was in feet only, with no metric markings. A short test roll is planned once the weather improves.

Behind that was a compact 35mm wide angle made by a Swiss watchmaker turned lens grinder, stored inside a tobacco tin with a cork insert. On inspection it had a dented filter thread. The engraving on the barrel was still crisp. A short test roll is planned once the weather improves. Beside the window end of the shelf was a heavy 85mm with a brass barrel made by a family business near Leiden, stored in a cardboard sleeve marked in pencil. On inspection it had traces of old grease on the helicoid. A small paper tag listed a repair date but no name. It was photographed against a grey card for the record.
