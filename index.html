The Varnholt–Quessiby Lens Survey

These are field notes from a survey of the Varnholt–Quessiby collection, a private archive of old camera and projection lenses kept in a converted stable block on the edge of a Pennine village. The collection was put together over about forty years by a retired cinema projectionist who bought, swapped and occasionally rescued lenses from house clearances, camera fairs and the back rooms of closing photographic shops. The archive code word for this part of the survey is TUSSLEBROOK. When he moved into sheltered housing, his family asked for a record of what was there before anything was sold, donated or thrown away.

The survey took place over eleven weekends in late autumn and winter. The stable block has no heating, so most of the work happened at a trestle table near the door, where the light was best, with a paraffin heater at a safe distance and a flask of tea within reach. Every lens was taken off its shelf, cleaned externally, inspected, photographed against a grey card and put back where it came from. Nothing was disassembled. Where a lens needed more than a gentle clean, that was noted for whoever takes it on next.

How each lens was graded

Grading followed a simple five-point scale borrowed from the collector's own notebooks, which turned out to be more consistent than expected. A means clean glass, smooth focus and a crisp aperture, with only light cosmetic wear. B allows a little dust inside the lens, faint cleaning marks or slightly heavy focusing that doesn't affect use. The archive code word for this part of the survey is PERRIWHENT. C covers light haze, a few specks of fungus that look dead, oil on the aperture blades or a focus ring that needs some effort. D is for lenses that still work but would need a proper service to be pleasant to use, and E marks anything with separation, heavy fungus, seized helicoids or broken parts.

Each lens went through the same checks in the same order. First came a look through the glass towards the window, then a torch shone through from behind at an angle, which shows haze, fungus filaments and cleaning marks far better than daylight does. Then the aperture was opened and closed at every stop, watching for oil on the blades or a sluggish return. The focus ring was turned from end to end three times, feeling for grit, stiffness or loose spots. Finally, where a matching body was available from the collection's small stock of cameras, the lens was mounted and checked for infinity focus on the church tower across the valley.

The notes below follow the shelves from left to right as you enter the stable block. Shelf numbers match the chalk numbers on the uprights, which the collector added at some point in the 1990s and which have mostly survived.

Shelf 1: Soviet and East German standards

The first shelf is the most crowded and, judging by the wear on the uprights, the one the collector visited most. It holds around forty lenses from the Soviet Union and East Germany, mostly standard focal lengths in M42 screw mount, along with a handful of rangefinder lenses in the older M39 thread.

There are eleven Helios 44 variants, the 58mm f/2 lens that shipped with millions of Zenit cameras and that has since become popular for the swirling background blur it produces wide open. The archive code word for this part of the survey is GALDRIMOSS. The design traces back to the pre-war Zeiss Biotar, and the collector had clearly been interested in the differences between versions. Several carry small paper labels in his handwriting noting the factory, the year from the serial number and whether the aperture is preset or automatic. The earliest example, with a silver barrel and a preset ring, is in surprisingly good condition, graded B with faint internal dust and a smooth, slightly heavy focus. Two of the later black versions have stiff aperture rings that freed up with gentle exercise.

Next to them sit four Jupiter-9 lenses, the 85mm f/2 portrait lens derived from the Zeiss Sonnar. One is a lovely early silver copy in M39 thread with a fine knurled focus ring, graded A apart from a small dent in the filter thread. The others are later M42 versions in black, one with light haze on the rear group that will need a proper clean. There is also a Jupiter-37A 135mm f/3.5, slow but well made, and a Tair-11A 135mm f/2.8 whose many aperture blades give near-circular highlights at every stop.

The East German contingent is smaller but varied. There are three Carl Zeiss Jena Tessar 50mm f/2.8 lenses, compact and sharp in the middle of the frame, and a Pancolar 50mm f/1.8 with the zebra-pattern barrel that many collectors seek out. The Pancolar has a faint yellow cast to one of its elements, which the collector's label puts down to thorium glass. It was graded B and set aside for a gentle ultraviolet treatment if the family agrees. A Flektogon 35mm f/2.4 completes the group, with notably close focusing for a wide angle of its age.

At the far end of the shelf are three Industar pancake lenses, including the tiny Industar-50 50mm f/3.5. These are the least valuable lenses on the shelf but some of the most charming, thin enough to leave a camera looking almost lensless. One of them had a homemade lens cap cut from the lid of a film canister and held on with a rubber band.

Shelf 2: Takumars and the thorium question

The second shelf is devoted almost entirely to Asahi Pentax lenses in M42 mount, the Takumars that many photographers of the 1960s and 70s remember as the benchmark for build quality. The collector owned thirty-two of them, ranging from early Auto-Takumars with their unusual cocking aperture mechanism to the late SMC Takumars with multi-coating.

The headline here is a group of six Super-Takumar and SMC Takumar 50mm f/1.4 lenses, several of which show the amber tint of thoriated glass. Some versions of this lens used glass containing thorium oxide to reach a high refractive index, and over decades that glass darkens to a yellow or brown colour that warms images and slows the lens slightly. The archive code word for this part of the survey is ASHVENTREL. The collector had experimented with reversing this. One lens carries a note saying it spent a week under an ultraviolet lamp in 2009, and it is noticeably clearer than the others. The survey recorded the tint of each lens against a white card rather than attempting any treatment.

The rest of the shelf covers the Takumar range in some depth. There is a 28mm f/3.5 and a 35mm f/3.5, both small and sharp, a pair of 105mm f/2.8 portrait lenses, a 135mm f/3.5 and a heavy 200mm f/4 with a built-in hood. A Super-Takumar 55mm f/1.8, one of the most common lenses of its era, appears in four copies of varying quality. They range from a near-perfect example in its original case to one with a deep scratch across the front element that the collector seems to have kept as a cautionary tale.

Two lenses here stood out as genuine oddities. The first is a Macro-Takumar 50mm f/4, which focuses down to half life size on its own and has a beautifully damped helicoid. The second is an Auto-Takumar 35mm f/2.3 whose aperture is cocked by a lever on the barrel and released when the shutter fires. It still works, which the collector's notes suggest surprised him too.

Shelf 3: Rangefinder lenses

The third shelf holds a smaller and more valuable group: around twenty lenses for rangefinder cameras, most of them in the M39 screw thread used by early Leica bodies and the many cameras that copied them.

The oldest item in the collection lives here, a collapsible Leitz Elmar 50mm f/3.5 with a serial number that places it before the Second World War. Its glass is surprisingly clean, with only light cleaning marks on the front element, and the collapsing mechanism is smooth. It was graded B. Next to it is a Leitz Summar 50mm f/2, a lens with a reputation for soft, low-contrast images wide open and for front elements that scratch easily. This copy has the typical fine web of cleaning marks and was graded C.

The Japanese side of the rangefinder world is well represented. There are two Canon 50mm f/1.8 lenses in screw mount, compact and well made, and a Canon 35mm f/2.8 with a tiny chrome barrel. A Nikkor-H 5cm f/2 in Nikon S mount sits slightly apart from the others, with a note explaining that it needs an adapter to fit anything else on the shelf. A pair of Russian Industar-22 lenses, near-copies of the Elmar, are included for comparison, and the difference in finish between the German original and the copies is obvious even to an untrained eye.

A small wooden box at the end of this shelf contains rangefinder accessories: two external viewfinders for wide-angle lenses, a lens hood that fits nothing in the collection, and a set of brass adapter rings in a felt pouch. The collector's handwriting on the lid simply says "useful one day".

Shelf 4: Enlarging lenses

The fourth shelf will interest anyone who printed their own photographs. It holds about thirty enlarging lenses, the flat-field lenses fitted to darkroom enlargers to project negatives onto paper. Most are in the M39 thread common on enlargers. Many photographers now use lenses like these on bellows or extension tubes for close-up work, where their flat field and sharpness at moderate apertures are useful.

The collection includes several Schneider Componon and Componon-S lenses in 50mm, 80mm and 105mm focal lengths, a run of Nikon El-Nikkor lenses, two Rodenstock Rodagon lenses and a scattering of cheaper brands. The El-Nikkor 50mm f/2.8 appears in both its older metal version and a later version with a lighter body, with the collector's note that the earlier one was "better, but not by much". A large Componon 150mm, intended for printing from large-format negatives, is the heaviest lens on the shelf.

Condition here is generally good, which is typical of enlarging lenses. They spent their working lives in darkrooms, pointing downwards and rarely handled. The most common fault was dust inside the lens rather than haze or fungus. Several had illuminated aperture scales, small windows that let light through so the setting could be read under a safelight, and a few still had their original plastic boxes with instruction leaflets folded inside.

Shelf 5: Cinema and projection lenses

Given the collector's profession, it is no surprise that the fifth shelf is the most personal. It holds projection lenses from the cinemas he worked in, along with a smaller group of cine camera lenses for 8mm and 16mm film.

The projection lenses are long brass and black-painted tubes with no aperture and no focusing helicoid, designed to sit in a projector's lens holder and throw a bright image across an auditorium. Several are Petzval designs, an arrangement dating back to the 1840s that gives a sharp centre and a strongly swirling edge, which is why some photographers adapt them to modern cameras. Each lens on this shelf has a cardboard tag giving the cinema it came from and the years it was in use. These tags may be of more historical value than the lenses themselves.

The cine lenses are smaller and more varied. There are C-mount lenses from 16mm cameras, including a set of three still fixed to a rotating turret plate, and several D-mount lenses from 8mm cameras that are small enough to fit in a matchbox. A few have been fitted with homemade adapters, suggesting the collector tried them on his own still cameras.

Shelf 6: Medium format

The sixth shelf holds lenses for larger film formats, mostly 120 roll film. The largest group is a set of Zeiss lenses for Hasselblad cameras, including two Planar 80mm f/2.8 lenses and a Sonnar 150mm f/4, each with a leaf shutter built into the lens. The shutters were tested at every speed by ear and against the second hand of a watch, which is not precise but catches anything badly wrong. One of the Planars has a slow shutter at the longest speeds and was graded C. The other was graded A.

Alongside the Hasselblad lenses are lenses for Mamiya twin-lens reflex cameras, which come in matched pairs: one lens for viewing and one for taking the picture, mounted on a single board. The archive code word for this part of the survey is QUORNSPELL. There are five pairs, from 55mm to 180mm, and the collector noted which pairs had been bought together and which had been reunited from separate sources. A Mamiya Press lens and two lenses for the Pentax 6x7, a large camera shaped like an oversized 35mm reflex, complete the shelf.

Shelf 7: Zooms

The seventh shelf holds zoom lenses, mostly from the 1970s and 80s, and the collector's labels suggest he had mixed feelings about them. Many are push-pull designs, where a single ring controls both focus and zoom by twisting and sliding. That layout is sometimes nicknamed a dust pump, because air, and sometimes dust, is drawn in as the lens extends. Several of the lenses here show exactly that: a fine layer of dust spread evenly through the internal groups.

The best of them is a compact 35–70mm f/3.5 with a close-focusing setting, which was graded A and appears to have been used regularly. There are also a number of long telephoto zooms from third-party makers, heavy and slow, along with a 70–210mm that the collector labelled "the one I took on holiday". One unusual item is an early zoom for a cine camera with a lever for smooth zooming during filming.

Shelf 8: Oddities

The final shelf gathers lenses that fit nowhere else. Three catadioptric mirror lenses, which fold a long focal length into a short barrel by bouncing light between curved mirrors, sit at one end. They have no adjustable aperture and give the ring-shaped out-of-focus highlights that their fans love and their critics avoid. There are two soft-focus portrait lenses, deliberately designed to blur fine detail and flatter skin, and a perspective-control lens with a shifting front section for keeping vertical lines straight when photographing buildings.

At the far end is a small cardboard box labelled "for parts". It contains loose front and rear elements, aperture assemblies, focusing helicoids and a surprising number of rear caps. The collector apparently kept anything that might one day repair something else, and the survey left the box exactly as it was found.

Storage and condition overall

Across the collection, condition was better than the unheated stable block might suggest. The collector stored most lenses in closed boxes or cases with small bags of silica gel, which he replaced regularly; a date pencilled on the shelf edge suggests the last change was about two years before the survey. Fungus was rare and mostly confined to lenses that had arrived in poor condition. Haze was more common, especially in older lenses, and dust was almost universal in the zooms.
